## Why Client-Side Matters

Reaction time is one of the simplest measures of how fast your brain and hand work together — and it matters in gaming, driving, and sport. Most reaction tests are buried in pages full of ads or ask you to sign up before you can play. This one runs entirely in your browser: a box that turns from red to green, your click, and your time in milliseconds. Nothing is installed, nothing is uploaded, and your results stay on your own device.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Testing your reaction takes a couple of minutes. First, click anywhere in the testing box to begin. Second, wait patiently while the screen is red — the green signal comes after a random delay, so you cannot predict it. Third, the instant the screen turns green, click as fast as possible. Fourth, repeat for a total of five rounds. Your average reaction time in milliseconds is calculated from all five rounds, which smooths out one lucky or unlucky click.

## Feature Deep Dive

The test is built around five rounds because a single click is noisy — one fast click might be luck, and one slow click might be a distraction, but the average of five gives a much steadier picture of your real speed. The delay before the box turns green is random each round, so you cannot learn the timing and click early; you have to genuinely react to the colour change. Average human visual reaction speed is roughly 250 milliseconds, and scores under 200 milliseconds are considered top-tier and fast — the kind of numbers competitive gamers aim for. Clicking while the box is still red triggers a false start warning, which keeps the test honest: guessing early does not count, and the round restarts. Because the whole test runs locally, your times are measured by your own device's clock with no network delay involved.

## Use Cases

Reaction tests are a favourite benchmark for gamers, who compare their milliseconds the way runners compare their times — and practise to shave a few milliseconds off their average. Athletes use them to check their reflexes before a season, and drivers use them as a rough check of alertness. They are also a fun challenge between friends, a quick brain-training break during a study session, and a simple way to see whether you are sharper in the morning or the evening. Some people track their average over weeks to see whether sleep, exercise, or practice makes a measurable difference to how fast they react.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why five rounds instead of just one?**

One click can be lucky or unlucky. Averaging five rounds smooths out the noise and gives a steadier, more honest measure of your real reaction speed.

**Does the time of day affect my score?**

Yes, it can. Most people react faster when they are alert and rested, and slower when they are tired. Try the test at different times and compare your averages.

**Can I improve my reaction time with practice?**

To a degree, yes. Regular practice and good sleep help, and many gamers find their average improves over weeks of short sessions. But there is a natural limit — nobody reacts in 50 milliseconds.

**What if I keep getting false starts?**

Slow down and wait for the green. The delay is random, so guessing the timing will keep failing — watch the box and react to the colour change itself.
