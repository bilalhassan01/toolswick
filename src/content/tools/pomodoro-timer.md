## Why Client-Side Matters

Staying focused for hours is hard, but staying focused for twenty-five minutes is something almost anyone can do. That is the idea behind the Pomodoro Technique: short, timed sprints of work broken up by real breaks. Many focus timers live inside apps that want an account, a subscription, or your data. This one runs entirely in your browser — a clean timer with a bell at the end of each sprint, no account, no tracking, and nothing to install.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Starting a Pomodoro session takes one click. First, click "Start" to begin a focused work session — 25 minutes by default, which you can change in the duration settings. Second, focus strictly on a single task until the bell rings, and resist the urge to check your phone or switch tasks. Third, take a short break of 5 minutes by default: stretch, get water, and rest your eyes. Fourth, repeat the cycle — after four focus rounds, take a longer break of 15 minutes by default before starting again.

## Feature Deep Dive

The timer follows the classic Pomodoro structure: a 25-minute work sprint, a 5-minute short break, and a 15-minute long break after every four completed rounds. The rhythm matters more than the exact numbers — the work sprint is short enough to stay focused, the short break is long enough to reset without losing momentum, and the long break rewards a full set of four sprints. All three durations can be customised in the settings, so if 25 minutes feels too long or too short for your work, you can adjust it. When each interval ends, an audio chime generated by the Web Audio API sounds — no media files to download, no buffering — so you hear the bell even if you are looking away from the screen.

## Use Cases

Pomodoro timers are used by students revising for exams, who break a long study session into sprints with real breaks instead of grinding until they burn out. Writers use them to draft in focused bursts, developers use them to work through a bug without drifting into distractions, and remote workers use them to structure a home workday that otherwise has no natural rhythm. It also works for household jobs: twenty-five minutes of cleaning, then a break, feels far more doable than an open-ended tidy-up. Anyone who struggles with procrastination finds the small commitment — just one sprint — easier to start than a whole afternoon of work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What if I finish my task before the 25 minutes are up?**

Use the remaining time to review your work, tidy up loose ends, or plan the next task. Or take your break early and start the next sprint — the timer is a guide, not a rule.

**Can I use the Pomodoro timer for something other than work?**

Yes. It works for studying, writing, cleaning, practising an instrument, or any task that benefits from focused bursts with regular breaks.

**What happens if I get interrupted during a sprint?**

Pause the timer if you can, deal with the interruption, and restart the sprint. One interrupted sprint is not a failure — the technique is about building a rhythm over the day.

**Do I have to take the breaks?**

The breaks are part of the method, not a reward for finishing. Skipping them leads to fatigue, and the next sprint suffers — so take the short break, even if it feels unnecessary.
