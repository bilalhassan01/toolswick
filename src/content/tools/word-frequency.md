## Why Client-Side Matters

Word frequency analysis usually means handing your text to someone else: an essay, an article draft, a speech, or a report you have not published yet. This tool works differently. Your text is counted and ranked entirely inside your own browser, on your device. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored on a server, and nothing remains behind after you close the tab. You can analyse a confidential draft or an unpublished article with the same confidence as a public one, because the words never leave your machine.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Analysing a text here takes less than a minute. First, paste your essay, article, or transcript into the input box at the top of the page. Second, set your options: the stop-words checkbox is ticked by default, which leaves out common grammatical words so the table shows the words that carry meaning, and the minimum word length box, set to two by default, lets you filter out very short words. Third, click Analyse Frequency. The tool shows two headline numbers, the total words analysed and the number of unique words, followed by a ranked table listing each word with its count and its frequency as a percentage. A Download CSV button saves the full table for use in a spreadsheet.

## Feature Deep Dive

The analysis counts every word in your text, then ranks them from most frequent to least. Words are matched without regard to capitalisation, so "The" at the start of a sentence and "the" in the middle count as the same word, which keeps the ranking honest. The stop-words filter removes high-frequency grammatical words such as "a", "the", and "is", which carry little topical information, so the top of the table shows the words that actually define what your text is about. The minimum word length setting works alongside it, letting you exclude one-letter words or set a higher floor for longer documents.

The results table shows four columns for each word: its rank, the word itself, how many times it appears, and its frequency as a percentage of all the words analysed. The percentage is what makes the table genuinely useful, because it turns raw counts into proportions you can compare across documents of different lengths. The CSV download contains the complete table, so you can sort, filter, or chart it in a spreadsheet.

## Use Cases

Frequency analysis is a quiet workhorse for writers, editors, and search specialists. Writers use it to spot repetition they did not notice: a favourite word appearing far more often than intended, or a phrase that keeps creeping back into a draft. Editors check whether a piece leans too heavily on a handful of words, and whether the vocabulary feels varied enough for the reader. Search specialists examine keyword density, checking how often a target term appears relative to the whole text, and whether related terms show up often enough to support the topic.

It is useful beyond professional writing too. Students analyse essays and speeches to see which ideas dominate their argument. Researchers scan interview transcripts to find the terms participants return to most. Anyone preparing a talk can check that the words they want the audience to remember are actually the words they used most.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How is the frequency percentage calculated?**

The percentage for each word is its count divided by the total number of words analysed, multiplied by one hundred. A word that appears ten times in a two-hundred-word text has a frequency of five percent, which lets you compare word use across documents of different lengths.

**Why do my total and unique word counts differ?**

The total is every word in your text, while the unique count is how many different words appear. A text of five hundred words might contain only two hundred unique ones, because common words repeat.

**What does the minimum word length setting do?**

It excludes words shorter than the number you choose. With the default of two, single-letter words are left out; raising it to four, for example, keeps only words of four letters or more in the table, which is useful when you want to focus on substantial terms.

**Does capitalisation affect the counts?**

No. Words are matched without regard to capital letters, so "Marketing" and "marketing" count as the same word. That keeps the ranking accurate for words that appear both at the start of sentences and in the middle of them.

**Does the tool keep my text after I analyse it?**

No. The analysis runs entirely in your browser. Your text is never uploaded or stored, and closing the tab leaves nothing behind. The CSV download is created on your device from the results you already have.
