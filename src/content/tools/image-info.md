## Why Client-Side Matters

Before you print a photo, upload it to a website, or send it to a client, it helps to know exactly what you are working with — its dimensions, its file size, and whether it is sharp enough for the job. Most inspection tools ask you to upload the image to a server just to read those numbers. This tool reads the details entirely inside your browser. Your image never leaves your device, nothing is stored on a server, and the results appear the moment the file is dropped in.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Checking an image takes seconds. First, drop any image or photo into the inspector box, or click to browse and select it from your device. Second, the details appear immediately: resolution in width by height, total megapixels, and aspect ratio, along with the file size and MIME type. Third, review the print dimensions at 300 DPI, shown in both inches and centimetres, plus the decoded RAM memory the image would use when opened for editing. Finally, click "Copy Specifications" to copy the full summary to your clipboard, ready to paste into an email, a listing, or a document.

## Feature Deep Dive

The inspector reports everything that matters about an image in one view. Resolution tells you how many pixels wide and tall the picture is, and megapixels — width times height divided by one million — give you a single number for how much detail it holds. The aspect ratio shows the shape of the image, such as 4:3, 16:9, or square, which decides how it will fit into frames, slides, and social posts. The print size at 300 DPI is the most practical number here: it tells you how large the image can be printed before it starts looking soft, because commercial printing needs about 300 dots per inch for crisp results. The RAM figure is equally useful for editors — when an image is decoded, each pixel typically takes 4 bytes of memory, so a large photo can use far more memory than its file size suggests.

## Use Cases

Image details matter whenever a picture has to meet a specification. Photographers check print sizes before ordering large prints, so they never pay for a poster that comes out blurry. Designers verify dimensions before placing images into layouts, and website owners check file sizes to keep pages fast. Sellers confirm their product photos meet a marketplace's minimum resolution before uploading a listing. It is also handy for everyday questions: finding out whether a phone photo is good enough to frame, checking why an image looks stretched in a presentation, or confirming the format of a file someone sent you before you try to open it in an editor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does my image look fine on screen but blurry when printed?**

Screens show images at low density, while print needs about 300 DPI. A photo that fills a screen can still be too small for a large print — check the 300 DPI print size here before ordering.

**What does the aspect ratio tell me?**

It describes the shape of the image, such as wide 16:9 or square 1:1. It helps you predict how the picture will fit into frames, slides, and social media formats without awkward cropping.

**How big is a megapixel, really?**

One megapixel is one million pixels. A 12-megapixel phone photo, for example, holds about 12 million pixels of detail, which is plenty for most prints up to a moderate size.

**Can I check several images one after another?**

Yes. Drop in a new image and the inspector updates instantly with its details. Use "Copy Specifications" each time to save the numbers you need.
