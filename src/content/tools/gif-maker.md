## Why Client-Side Matters

Animated GIFs are still one of the simplest ways to share a short loop — a reaction, a quick demo, or a few frames of a drawing coming together. Most GIF makers upload your frames to a server, where they can be stored, scanned, or watermarked. This tool compiles your GIF entirely inside your browser, using the canvas and native encoding built into the page. Your frames never leave your device, nothing is stored on a server, and the finished animation downloads straight to you.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Making an animated GIF takes only a few minutes. First, upload two or more image frames — PNG, JPG, or WebP — in the order you want them to play. Second, adjust the frame delay in milliseconds to set the playback speed, and set the output width if you want the GIF smaller than the original frames. Third, click "Create Animated GIF" and the tool compiles the frames locally into a single animation. Finally, preview the looping animation on the page to check the speed and order, then download your .gif file.

## Feature Deep Dive

The GIF is assembled frame by frame in browser memory, with each image drawn onto a canvas and encoded into the GIF format with its standard looping behaviour. The frame delay control is the heart of the tool: a short delay makes a snappy, fast animation, while a longer delay slows the loop down so each frame can be read — for example, a slideshow of diagrams or a step-by-step demo. The output width setting lets you shrink the whole animation for sharing in chats or on pages where a large file would be slow to load. Because the frames are compiled locally, there is no upload queue and no waiting for a server to process your images, and you can rebuild the GIF as many times as you like after changing the order, the speed, or the size.

## Use Cases

GIFs are everywhere in everyday communication. People turn a burst of photos into a looping reaction image for group chats, or stitch together screenshots to show a friend how to do something step by step. Designers animate a logo or a simple illustration to bring a social post to life, and teachers build short loops that show a process — a plant growing, a diagram assembling, or a maths step unfolding — far more clearly than a single still image. Small businesses use GIFs for product demos and announcements, because a moving image catches the eye in a feed where static pictures blend together. They also work well in presentations, where a short loop can illustrate a point without needing a video file.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many frames do I need to make a GIF?**

You need at least two frames. More frames make a smoother animation, but each extra frame also makes the file larger, so a short loop of a handful of frames usually works best.

**Why does my GIF look choppy?**

Choppy playback usually means the frame delay is too long or there are too few frames. Try shortening the delay so frames change faster, or add intermediate frames to smooth the motion.

**Can I change the speed after creating the GIF?**

Yes. Just adjust the frame delay and click "Create Animated GIF" again — your frames stay loaded, so rebuilding with a new speed takes seconds.

**What size should I make the GIF for sharing online?**

Smaller widths load faster in chats and feeds. Keep the original width for a presentation or a website hero, and use a reduced width when the GIF is going into a message or a social post.
