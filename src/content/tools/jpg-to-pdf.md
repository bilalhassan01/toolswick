## Why Client-Side Matters

Photos and scans are easy to take but awkward to submit. Government portals, universities, banks, and employers usually demand documents in PDF format rather than raw image files, and a folder of loose JPGs is not a submission. Most online converters ask you to upload those images to a server you do not control, where copies of personal documents — ID cards, certificates, medical records — can sit in storage or pass through logs. This tool works differently. Your images are combined into a PDF entirely inside your own browser using the pdf-lib engine, in memory, on your device. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored on a server, and nothing remains behind after you close the tab.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Turning images into a PDF here takes less than a minute, and every step happens on the same page. First, drop your images into the workspace, or click to browse and select them from your computer or phone. You can mix JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF, and BMP images in a single document. Second, choose a paper size — A4, US Letter, or Fit to Image — and arrange the images in the order you want them to appear, with one image per page. Fit to Image keeps each picture at its natural dimensions instead of fitting it onto a standard page. Third, click "Create PDF" to build the document and download it to your device.

## Feature Deep Dive

The conversion engine is pdf-lib, an open-source library that runs in your browser and assembles a real PDF document from your images. Each image is placed on its own page, so a set of scanned form pages becomes a multi-page PDF.

Three paper sizes cover the common cases. A4 suits most submissions outside North America, US Letter suits American portals and offices, and Fit to Image keeps each picture at its natural dimensions — the right choice for a wide receipt, a tall certificate, or a photo that would look wrong squeezed onto a standard page. The page order follows the order you set, so a scanned form reads front to back. There is no server-imposed limit — the only practical limit is your device's browser memory. Your original images are never changed.
## Use Cases

Converting images to PDF is one of those small tasks that comes up constantly once you notice it. The most common case is a submission: a visa portal, a university application, or a bank asks for a document as a single PDF, and the only copy you have is a set of phone photos of the paper original. Scanning a signed contract page by page and combining the images into one PDF gives you a file that uploads cleanly and reads in order.

It is also the simplest way to share a set of images as a document. A portfolio of photos, a set of handwritten notes, or the pages of a notebook all become a single file that anyone can open without special software. Because each image gets its own page in the order you choose, the result looks deliberate — and since the conversion happens entirely on your device, even sensitive images never leave your hands.
## Frequently Asked Questions

**What paper size should I choose?**

A4 is the safe default for most submissions outside North America, and US Letter suits American portals and offices. Choose Fit to Image when the picture itself matters — a wide receipt or tall certificate — because it keeps the image at its natural dimensions instead of fitting it onto a standard page.

**Will my images lose quality in the PDF?**

No. Your images are placed into the PDF as they are, rather than being re-photographed or squeezed through a screenshot, so the picture you put in is the picture that comes out.

**Can I reorder the images after adding them?**

Yes. You can change the order of the images before you create the PDF, so a scanned form reads front to back and a set of photos appears in the sequence you want.

**Does each image get its own page?**

Yes. The tool puts one image on each page of the PDF, so a ten-image batch becomes a clean ten-page document that reads like a proper file.

**How many images can I convert at once?**

There is no fixed limit. The tool does not cap the number of images, because it does not process anything on a server. The practical limit is your browser's available memory, which comfortably handles ordinary batches of photos and scans.
