## Why Client-Side Matters

Search engines reward content that uses its target topic naturally, and they penalise pages that repeat the same keyword until the text reads like it was written for a robot. Checking that balance should not mean handing your unpublished draft to a server you do not control. An unpublished draft is your work, and uploading it to an online analyser puts a copy somewhere you cannot see. This tool runs the whole analysis inside your own browser. Your text never leaves your device, never sits in a server log, and never becomes training data for someone else's product.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Start by pasting your article, blog post, or page copy into the workspace. The tool counts the words and builds its tables immediately, with no button to press. Next, adjust the settings if you need to: stop word exclusion is on by default so words like "the" and "and" do not crowd the results, and you can raise the minimum word length to filter out short fragments. Then read the three tables: single keywords, two-word phrases, and three-word phrases, each with its count and density percentage. Any keyword that looks overused is flagged with a stuffing alert, so you can rewrite that passage before publishing.

## Feature Deep Dive

The analyser breaks your text into one-word, two-word, and three-word phrases, known as n-grams, and counts how often each one appears. Density is the share of total words: a keyword that appears 12 times in a 600-word article has a density of 2%. Each table is sorted by frequency, so the most repeated terms sit at the top. Stop words are filtered by default because they carry no topical meaning, and the minimum word length setting lets you drop short words that add noise to the results. The stuffing alert is the practical heart of the tool. As a rough guide, 1% to 2.5% reads as natural coverage, while a much higher figure suggests the keyword is being forced.

## Use Cases

Blog writers use it as a final check before hitting publish, confirming the target keyword appears often enough to signal the topic without dominating the page. SEO specialists run competitor copy through it to see which terms a ranking page leans on, then compare that against their own draft. Freelance writers use it to prove to a client that a brief's keyword requirements were met without stuffing. It also helps with product descriptions and landing pages, where short text makes accidental repetition easy. Anyone editing a long guide can use the phrase tables to spot accidental repetition of a clumsy three-word phrase that slipped through several sections.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What keyword density should I aim for?**

Around 1% to 2.5% is a common rough guide, but there is no magic number. A short page will naturally show higher density than a long one, and what matters most is that the text reads naturally. Use the figure as a warning light, not a target to hit exactly.

**Does the tool save or upload my text?**

No. The analysis runs entirely in your browser, so your draft never leaves your device. You can check client work, unpublished articles, and paid content without worrying about where a copy might end up.

**Why do some ordinary words still appear in the results?**

The stop word filter removes the most common filler words, but meaningful short words can still show up. Raise the minimum word length setting to hide them, or simply ignore terms that are part of normal sentences rather than topical keywords.

**Can I check a competitor's page with this tool?**

Yes. Paste in any text you have the right to use, such as a competitor's public page copy, and the tool analyses it exactly the same way. It is a quick way to see which terms a ranking page repeats most.
