## Why Client-Side Matters

Barcodes turn a product code, SKU, or asset number into something a scanner can read in a fraction of a second. Many barcode websites generate the image on a remote server, which means your product codes and inventory numbers travel across the internet and may be logged. This generator builds every barcode inside your own browser, on your device. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and your codes stay private from the first character to the final download.

## Step-by-Step Guide

Making a barcode here takes less than a minute. First, type your content into the text box — a SKU, product code, asset number, or any short piece of text. Second, pick a standard: Code 128 for most uses, or Code 39 when you need a simpler alphanumeric code. Third, adjust the bar height and scale to suit your label, choose your bar and background colours, and decide whether the human-readable text should appear underneath. The preview updates as you work, so you can see exactly what you will print. Finally, download your barcode as a PNG image or as a scalable SVG file.

## Feature Deep Dive

Code 128 is the workhorse of the two standards. It is dense, compact, and encodes the full ASCII set, so it fits long codes into a short strip — the reason it is the usual choice for retail and logistics. Code 39 is an older, simpler standard that handles uppercase letters, digits, and a small set of symbols, and it remains common on warehouse shelves and industrial labels. Bar height runs from 40 to 200 pixels, so you can make a short code for a small label or a tall one that scans easily from a distance. The scale setting runs from 1x compact to 4x high-resolution, which matters when a barcode will be printed small or on a rough surface. You can also change the bar and background colours, though black bars on a white background scan most reliably. PNG suits quick printing and documents, while SVG stays perfectly sharp at any size, which is what professional packaging work needs.

## Use Cases

Small shops use barcodes to label products, price stock, and speed up the checkout till, while online sellers print them on parcels and packing slips so orders can be tracked through every step. Warehouses label bins, shelves, and pallets so pickers can scan instead of typing long codes by hand, which cuts errors. Offices and schools tag laptops, projectors, books, and other equipment so an asset register stays accurate. Event organisers print barcodes on tickets and wristbands for fast entry checks, and makers and craft sellers add them to handmade goods to give a small product a professional, retail-ready finish.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I change the bar height after generating a barcode?**

Yes. The height control runs from 40 to 200 pixels and the preview updates instantly, so you can try a taller or shorter barcode and download the version that fits your label best.

**What characters can I use in a Code 39 barcode?**

Code 39 handles uppercase letters, digits, and a small set of symbols such as the hyphen and full stop. If your code needs lowercase letters or a wider character range, choose Code 128 instead.

**Will my barcode still scan if I print it small?**

It should, as long as you keep the contrast high and the scale generous. Use the 2x or 3x scale, stick to dark bars on a light background, and always test-scan one printed label before printing a whole batch.

**Can I show the code text underneath the bars?**

Yes. The human-readable text label is switched on by default, so the code appears in plain text below the bars. Turn it off if you want a clean barcode with no text at all.
